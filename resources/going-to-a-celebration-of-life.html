The basics

What a celebration of life is

A celebration of life is a gathering to remember someone. It usually happens without the body there, often weeks or months after the death. It might be in a backyard, a restaurant, a park shelter, a church hall or a rented room.

"Celebration" doesn't mean everyone has to be happy. People laugh, and people cry, often in the same ten minutes. Both are welcome. You don't have to perform cheerfulness, and you don't have to hide it either.

Most have some mix of these: time to mingle, food, photos or objects from their life, music, and a time when people share stories. Some have a short program at a set time. Others are drop-in, where people come and go.

Clothes

What to wear

  • Read the invitation first. Families often say what they want: "casual," "wear something bright," "wear his Tigers gear." Do what they ask. It's part of how they're remembering the person.
  • If it says nothing, dress the way you would for a nice dinner out. Clean, comfortable, not flashy.
  • Black is fine. So is color. You don't have to wear black, and you won't stand out if you do.
  • Check the place. A park or backyard means real shoes and a layer for Michigan weather. A church hall or restaurant means a step dressier.
Gifts

What to bring

  • You don't have to bring anything. Showing up is the gift.
  • A card is always right. Write one specific memory or thing you loved about them. Families reread these for years.
  • Check before bringing flowers. There may not be a place to put them, and the family has to take them home after. If the invitation or obituary names a charity, give there instead.
  • Bring food only if asked. Some gatherings are potlucks, and the invitation will say so. Otherwise the food is planned.
  • Photos and stories are the best gifts. If you have a picture they've never seen, bring a printed copy or offer to send it. If there's a memory book or table, add to it.
  • Money is OK when it helps. If the family is struggling to cover costs, a check in a card or a gift to their fundraiser is kind and practical. Don't make a show of it.
Timing

When to arrive and how long to stay

  • If there's a program at a set time, arrive 10 to 15 minutes early so you're seated when it starts. Walking in during the stories is hard on everyone.
  • If it's drop-in, come any time in the window. Staying 30 minutes to an hour is normal. Close friends often stay longer.
  • Before you leave, find someone in the family and say goodbye. "Thank you for doing this" is enough.
  • Don't worry about leaving early if you need to. Nobody is keeping track.
Words

What to say to the family

You don't need the perfect words. Short and honest is better than polished.

Things that help:

  • "I'm so sorry."
  • "I'm really glad I knew her."
  • A specific memory: "He drove me to the airport at 5 a.m. and wouldn't take gas money."
  • Their name. Families want to hear it said out loud.
  • Nothing at all. A hug or sitting next to someone counts.

Things to skip:

  • "Everything happens for a reason," or anything starting with "at least."
  • "They're in a better place," unless you know the family believes that.
  • "I know how you feel." Tell them about your own loss another day, if they ask.
  • Questions about how they died, unless the family brings it up.
  • "Let me know if you need anything." It's kind, but it puts the work on them. Offer something specific instead: "Can I bring dinner Tuesday?"
Sharing

If you're asked to speak, or there's open sharing

  • Tell one story, not their whole life. Two or three minutes is plenty when other people want a turn.
  • Make it about them, not about you. The best stories show who they were: what they said, what they did, what they were like.
  • Funny is welcome. Laughter is one of the best parts of these gatherings.
  • Write it down and bring it, even if you don't read it. Nerves and grief make your mind go blank.
  • It's OK to cry while you talk. Take a breath and keep going. Everyone in the room understands.
Children

Bringing kids

Kids are usually welcome unless the invitation says otherwise. Being there can help them understand that someone died and that people loved them. Before you go, tell them what they'll see: people might cry, people might laugh, and both are OK. Bring something quiet for them to do, and step out if they need a break.

From a distance

If you can't go

  • Send a card or a message with a specific memory. It matters just as much.
  • Ask if it's being streamed or recorded. Many are now.
  • Send a photo or a story they can share at the gathering, if there's still time.
Later

After the day

The gathering is one afternoon. The grief keeps going. Most people stop checking in after a few weeks, which is often when the family feels most alone. Put a reminder on your calendar to reach out in a month, on their birthday, and on the anniversary of the death. A short text that says "thinking of you and of him today" is enough.
