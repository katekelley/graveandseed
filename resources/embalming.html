Many people are never told that embalming is a choice. In Michigan, the law rarely requires it. It doesn't protect anyone's health. And a funeral home can't do it without your permission. Here's what the rules say.

When Michigan law requires it

  • A few rare diseases. If the person died of diphtheria, a meningococcal infection, plague, polio, scarlet fever or smallpox, the body must be embalmed before it's moved.1
  • Shipping a body more than 48 hours after death. A state rule says a body can be shipped in a sealed case only if it will arrive within 48 hours of the death. Later than that, it must be embalmed. The rule is about shipping, with permits taped to the box. It isn't about a funeral home moving someone across town.2

That's all. No Michigan law requires embalming for a viewing, a funeral or a burial, or because a few days have passed. State law treats cooling as the normal way to hold a body. When family members disagree about plans, a funeral home may embalm only if cooling isn't available. Since 2025, Michigan also licenses cold-storage places that hold bodies before burial or cremation.9,10

One caution: the state licensing office reads the shipping rule much more broadly, as if a body that isn't embalmed can't be moved at all after 48 hours.1 In practice, funeral homes here often keep bodies cool for longer than that before cremation. That reading also doesn't fit the rest of Michigan law. If a funeral home cites it, ask them to show you the rule they mean.

They need your permission

  • Michigan law says a funeral director can't embalm a body unless a relative, or the person in charge of the plans, clearly says yes.1
  • Under the federal Funeral Rule, a funeral home can't charge you for embalming you didn't approve, unless the law required it.3

It doesn't protect public health

The Funeral Consumers Alliance says the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and health officials in Canada agree: embalming does nothing for public health. The chemicals are toxic enough that embalmers must wear masks that filter the air.4 Cooling is the usual choice for the days before a burial or cremation.

Why funeral homes ask for it anyway

The FTC notes that many funeral homes require embalming if the public will see the body, even though most states don't.3 The FTC also makes every price list say embalming "may be necessary" for some plans, like a viewing. That sounds like a law, even when it's just the funeral home's policy. In 2022, the FTC said it might change that wording so people understand how rarely embalming is needed.5

Funeral homes mostly want it so the body looks a certain way at a viewing several days after the death. What to ask instead

  • Can we see them privately, soon after the death, without embalming?
  • Do you keep bodies cool instead? What does that cost?
  • If we want a public viewing, can they be seen without embalming? If not, why not?
  • Will you help us keep them at home for a vigil?

Keeping them at home

Michigan is one of only a few states that requires a licensed funeral director to help care for the body and arrange the burial or cremation.6,7 You can still hold a vigil at home with a funeral director who's willing to work with you. People usually keep the body cool with dry ice or reusable cold packs, plus an open window or air conditioning. With good cooling, a home vigil often lasts up to about three days. Dry ice should never touch skin, and the room needs fresh air. Ask your funeral director or a home funeral guide to show you how.8
