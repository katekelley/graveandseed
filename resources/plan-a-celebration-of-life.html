A celebration of life is a gathering to remember someone, shaped around who they were. There are no rules for it. That's freeing, and it can also feel like a lot to figure out. Here's a simple way through.

Celebration of life, memorial or funeral?

  • A funeral usually happens within a week or two, with the body present, often led by a funeral home or clergy.
  • A memorial service happens without the body, and is often more formal or religious.
  • A celebration of life also happens without the body, and is usually more personal and relaxed. It can be anywhere, at any time, and it can include tears and laughter both.

These aren't legal categories. Call it whatever feels right, and mix the parts you want.

Start with three decisions

  1. Who's in charge. Pick one person to make the final calls, even if everyone helps. It saves a lot of group texts.
  2. When. After a cremation there's no rush. Many people wait a few weeks so people can travel, or pick a date that meant something: a birthday, an anniversary, or the season they loved.
  3. How many people. Make an honest guess, then plan for more. Celebrations of life often draw coworkers, neighbors and old friends you didn't know about.

Pick a place

  • Up to about 25 people: a home, a backyard, a favorite restaurant's back room.
  • 25 to 75: a park shelter, a church or meetinghouse hall, a library program room, a brewery or event room.
  • More than 75: an event venue, a large hall, or a garden or farm with indoor space for bad weather.

What happens during it

Most celebrations of life run two to three hours. A simple shape that works:

  1. Arrival (20 to 30 minutes): people find each other, look at photos and get a drink.
  2. A welcome from the host: a few words about why you're here.
  3. Planned remembrances: three or four people, about five minutes each. Ask them ahead of time, and tell them the time limit.
  4. Open stories: anyone who wants to can share a memory. Have one or two people ready to go first.
  5. Something everyone does together: a toast, lighting candles, a song, planting seeds, or writing notes for those closest to them.
  6. Food and time together, then a few closing words, so people know it's okay to leave.

Ideas that make it feel like them

  • A memory table with photos and their things: tools, recipe cards, a well-worn jacket, their mug.
  • A slideshow or a photo wall. Ask guests to bring or send photos ahead of time.
  • Their music. A playlist of their favorites, or a friend who plays.
  • Their food. Make their famous recipe, or have their favorite restaurant cater.
  • Cards where guests write a memory, gathered into a book to keep.
  • Something to take home: seed packets, a recipe card, a copy of a poem they loved.

Food

Food gives people a reason to stay and something to do with their hands. If the gathering runs through lunch or dinner, people will expect a meal; mid-afternoon or evening, lighter food is fine. A potluck is fine too. How much to order, costs per person and local caterers

Telling people

  • Use the channels they used. A Facebook event, a group text, an email to their coworkers, a note in their club's newsletter.
  • Include: their name, the date and time, the address, whether kids are welcome, what to wear, and where to send donations or what to do instead of flowers.
  • Say what to wear. People worry about this. "Come as you are," "wear something bright" or "wear your Michigan gear" all help.
  • Put the details in the obituary if you write one. How to write an obituary that sounds like them

If there was a cremation

  • The ashes can be there, or not. Some people place the urn on the memory table. Others keep the ashes private and plan a separate scattering later.
  • You can divide the ashes. Funeral homes can split them between family members, or into small keepsakes.
  • Scattering: Michigan doesn't have a state law about where you can scatter ashes. On private land, get the owner's permission. On public land, including parks and the national lakeshores, check the rules of whoever manages it first. Some require a permit.

A simple timeline

Print the Their celebration worksheet to plan on paper, with a run of show and a who-does-what list.

  1. Four or more weeks out: choose the person in charge, the date, the place and a budget.
  2. Three weeks out: send the invitation, ask your speakers, and book the food.
  3. One to two weeks out: gather photos, make the playlist and slideshow, buy or borrow supplies, and give the caterer a final count.
  4. The day before: confirm who brings what, and who's setting up.
  5. On the day: ask one friend, not the people closest to them, to run things: greet the caterer, keep time, and handle problems. Your job is to be there.
  6. Afterward: share photos with everyone who came, and send thank-you notes when you're ready. There's no deadline.

What it costs

A celebration of life can cost very little: a backyard, a potluck and a borrowed speaker. Most of the cost is usually the venue and the food. See a sample all-in budget, including cremation, for about 60 people.
