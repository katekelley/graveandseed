*This piece describes an embalming.*

Like many people, I remember being creeped out by bodies at funerals as a child. They looked weird, in a way we might now call a sort of uncanny valley effect. The heavy foundation on a great-grandparent who was always bare-faced, the stiff, set features, it all added up to someone I didn’t quite recognize.

In my experience with my own dying grandpa, I learned that there is an option between seeing someone in the last, hard moments of life and seeing them embalmed and made up in a funeral home.

My grandpa died at home, on hospice. Dying is hard work, and he looked tired, sweaty, and uncomfortable. After he died, my mom, my sister and I were able to care for his body. We bathed him, put him in fresh pajamas and warm socks, and gave him a shave.

We got to sit with him in the calm of a bright, snowy day. He looked more like himself than he had in a long time, a noble profile at rest.

He looked dead. He was cold and still, but also peaceful, a sort of total ease after the hard work of living and dying.

We’d had friends and family visit him as he was dying, to express their love and comfort him, and I wished more of them were able to come and sit with him after, to remember him as the man he once was and take comfort from that themselves.

After that experience, I wanted everyone to have the opportunity to sit with their loved ones made more recognizable, not less.

My opinion grew more nuanced as I witnessed an embalming for the first time. The person being cared for had been ill for a long time. They were in rough shape on the table. As they were washed and the initial embalming fluid was injected, I saw them gain some of that same easeful look my grandpa had. The embalming fluid had dye in it, so they also looked a little pinker, closer to sleeping than to death. In this moment I thought I saw what embalming could be and why it might comfort a family.

But the process didn’t stop there. Like a lot of people after a long illness, they had lost weight. Fillers were injected around their sunken temples and eyes. And rapidly, I thought they began to look different. Not like the healthy person in the pictures, and not like the gentle dead. Like something else, uncanny. Their mouth was sewn closed and their eyes glued shut, in the workmanlike manner of a professional. I thought how sad I would have been for those needles to be plunged by strangers into my grandfather’s body.

I think families deserve to know they don’t have to embalm to see their beloved dead, and that they can be embalmed but allowed to look more like themselves. The industry has an idea of the cosmetic dead, but it isn’t the only option available.

If you’re planning for someone you love, here’s what you can ask for. You can sit with them at home, or soon after the death, without embalming. If you do choose embalming, you can ask the funeral home to skip the fillers and keep the makeup light. Bring a photo, or their own makeup. Ask to see them before anyone else does, so you can ask for changes.

Dead people aren’t so scary — they’re just like us.
